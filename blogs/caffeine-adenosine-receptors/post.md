# Adenosin receptors. 

Hank Green's picture (as always) is well established in the literature. [This review article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9541543) by Reichert et al. Explains that caffeine both *blocks the promotion* of sleep-inducing processes in the brain and also *blocks the inhibition* of wakefulness promoters (Section 3, of Reichert et al).  Caffeine does this by being an antagonist to adenosine receptors ([4.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9541543/#jsr13597-sec-0004) Reichert et al). An antagonist is a molecule that binds to a specific receptor but does not cause that receptor, when bound, to induce the signal. So there's effectively less room for adenosine to act and do its signaling job. Adenosine slowly builds up while you are awake. It is what the community calls a driver of sleep pressure. Sleep pressure accumulates over the day, and at some particular point, it makes you feel sleepy. <Add citation for this> Back to my question, though: if this is all sleep pressure built during the day, why does coffee wake me up in the morning? Furthermore, what causes my super alertness? 

## What does normal Adenosine binding do? 

There are actually four types of adenosine receptors. They are what is called a G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCR) <cite>. I won't go into too much detail about what that means, but what's most relevant is how each type further propagates signals down the body. 
- A1 - inhibits cAMP.
- A2a - stimulates cAMP.
- A2b - stimulates cAMP.
- A3 - inhibits cAMP. 

cAMP is a secondary messaging molecule within cells. Its concentration is tied to the activation of a series of other molecules in a cell. Importantly for our story, this is cell-type-specific. A neuron that has higher cAMP values will behave differently to a skin cell that has higher cAMP values. It has to do with the type of proteins these cells express.

Across neurons, A1 and A2a are the most common adenosine receptors. A2a and A3 are also found within the brain, but generally in non-neurons, such as immune cells. At a very high level, adenosine, when bound to A1, inhibits the neuron's ability to fire. In A2A, the cAMP behaves *opposite*. When A2a is bound by adenosine, cAMP *increases*. A2A is mainly located in regions of the brain that, when activated, suppress motor movement <cite>. 

So A1 makes some neurons "harder to fire", and A2A enables other neurons to dampen that firing. Caffeine, by blocking adenosine, stops these effects from taking place.
