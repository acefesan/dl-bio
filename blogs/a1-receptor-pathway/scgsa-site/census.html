What is in the Census, and what does a zero mean there?

Before scoring anything on the whole CELLxGENE Census, we need to know what we are holding: which assays, which tissues, which genes, and what each kind of zero stands for. Every number on this page is computed from the local copy (build 2025-11-08) and each figure says how.

Scope decision

Everything on these four pages is restricted to 10x assays (any assay name starting with "10x": 3′ v1/v2/v3, 5′ v1/v2, multiome, Flex) and to primary cells (is_primary_data = True), so a cell re-published by several datasets is counted once. The normalisation is the same everywhere: the Census normalized layer, which is the raw count divided by the cell's total count. Multiply by 10,000 to get counts per 10,000 (CP10K).

2. The assays: 10x is nearly everything, and there are two others worth knowing

You remembered two other methods. From the data:

  • Smart-seq (Smart-seq, -seq2, -seq3, -seq v4 all together) is tiny: 0.16M of the 96.6M human primary cells, or about 0.17%. In mouse it is 0.19M of 18.4M (about 1%). Its draw is that it reads full-length transcripts, so it can see isoforms and variants, but it is slow and expensive per cell.
  • The other one I think you mean is sci-RNA-seq3 (combinatorial indexing, run on nuclei). It is 4.2M human primary cells (4.4%), but 11.4M of the 18.4M mouse primary cells (62%), because several whole-organism mouse atlases used it. So in mouse, "all of SOMA" is mostly not 10x. If that is not the method you had in mind, the full list is in the figure.

3. Cells versus nuclei, a confound that matters for ADORA

A large share of the 10x data is not cells but nuclei (single-nucleus RNA-seq), which brain tissue needs because neurons are hard to dissociate whole. Nuclei capture nuclear RNA: more unspliced transcripts and fewer cytoplasmic or mitochondrial ones. A receptor gene can look different in a nucleus than in a whole cell for reasons unrelated to biology, so the later pages let you split every result by this flag.

4. What 10x actually reads, and what reference it is counted against

Your reading is close, with one refinement. 10x does sequence a piece of each transcript, but only a short one. In the standard 3′ kits, one read carries the cell barcode and a molecule tag (UMI) and the other carries roughly 90 bases of cDNA from near the 3′ end of the transcript (the 5′ kits do the same from the 5′ end). That is enough to say which gene this molecule came from and to count it once per UMI. It is generally not enough to tell isoforms apart, and a sequence variant is visible only if it happens to sit inside that short window. The Flex chemistry is stricter still: it detects gene-specific probes, so you only learn about the targets the probe set covers, and no sequence at all. So the data supports "how much of gene X" and does not support polymorphism or isoform questions. Smart-seq, being full-length, is the Census's only real route to those, and it is under 0.2% of cells.

What is "the gene" in the Census? The Census does not store the submitters' own gene lists. Each dataset is curated to Ensembl gene IDs against a pinned annotation: for the schema version used by this build (7.0.0), human is GENCODE v48 (GRCh38) and mouse is GENCODE vM37 (GRCm39), according to the schema document. Submitters aligned their reads with whatever reference they had (a common default for 10x is an older GENCODE release), and the curation step maps those to the pinned IDs. The human gene table has 61,497 features of which only 20,080 are protein-coding; the rest are lncRNAs, pseudogenes, small RNAs and similar. Gene symbols such as ADORA1 are labels attached to those IDs, so "ADORA1" always means the same Ensembl gene (ENSG00000163485) across datasets, which is the standardisation you asked about. I have not audited how many genes were lost or remapped in that curation step.

5. Four kinds of zero

You suggested a zero can also mean "the gene was not even measured". That is real in the Census, and it is common enough to matter. The four meanings:

Zero means…What happenedCan we tell it apart?
Biological zeroThe gene is not expressed in this cell.Not from one cell. Only by comparing many similar cells.
Sampling zeroThe gene is expressed, but no molecule was captured.Same as above; the chance is about e−N·p (see Scoring from zero).
Not listedThe gene is not a column in this dataset at all, so it was never counted. The Census records this per dataset in its presence matrix.Yes, exactly. This page measures it.
Not capturableThe chemistry cannot see it: Flex's probe set leaves it out, or the dataset was a targeted panel.Mostly: these datasets have very short gene lists.

For 10x 3′ and 5′ the assay can in principle capture any polyadenylated transcript, so not listed usually reflects what the submitter kept (many filter out genes seen in only a few cells) rather than what the assay could see. Either way the zero is not evidence of absence: the cell never got a chance to report the gene. Figure 5a shows how short the gene lists are.

5a. How many genes each 10x dataset lists
Share of 10x primary cells (not datasets) whose dataset lists this many genes. Full table is 60,606 genes in human.
How this figure was made

Every cell in the Census carries n_measured_vars, the number of genes in its dataset's feature list. Taking the maximum of it per dataset (it is constant within a dataset) gives one value per dataset. Datasets are then binned by gene-list size and each bin shows the share of cells from datasets in that bin, so a 500,000-cell atlas counts for more than a 5,000-cell one. The shortest lists are almost all datasets whose authors filtered genes hard, or Flex probe sets (about 14,000 targets).

5b. Genes ranked by how many cells could have counted them
For each gene, the fraction of 10x primary cells whose dataset lists it. Bars are numbers of genes.
How this figure was made

The Census feature_dataset_presence_matrix is a (datasets × genes) table of ones for every gene a dataset lists. census_overview_data.py reads it whole and, for each gene, sums the 10x primary cell counts of the datasets that list it, divided by the total number of 10x primary cells. Genes are then binned by that fraction. The 11,059 human genes in the last bin are the ones almost every dataset lists; the 25,349 in "<25%" are mostly lncRNAs and pseudogenes that most datasets dropped.

6. Where the cells are

The same tissue picture as figure 3 sets the sampling bias for everything after it: brain and blood together are more than half (about 57%) of the human 10x cells. A statement such as "ADORA1 is expressed in X% of all cells" mostly describes brain and blood. That is why the next pages go tissue by tissue and cell type by cell type instead of reporting one pooled number.
