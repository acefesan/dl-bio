What does a gene set actually say about a cell?

The anchoring question for everything on this site. Before ranking methods or reading an A1 score, it helps to be exact about what a list of genes is, what a score computed from it can and cannot tell you, and which genes in the adenosine pathways a cell would need to have in the first place.

How this page was researched

You suggested handing the "why do gene sets make sense" question to a sub-agent. I did it directly instead, so the reasoning is in one place: the pharmacology comes from the sources linked in the text, the KEGG numbers were pulled live from KEGG, and the expression numbers come from the local Census. Anything I could not verify is marked.

1. What a gene set is, from first principles

A gene set is a list with a claim attached. The claim is "these genes take part in the same process", such as one signalling pathway. Curators wrote the list by reading the literature, once, for everyone. It does not know about your cells.

A score turns the list into one number per cell. The seven methods on this site differ in how, but all of them ask the same question: relative to some reference, how much of this list is turned up in this cell? The reference is what each method's page calls what it "compares against".

So the honest reading of a gene-set score is: "in this cell, the genes on this list are collectively detected more (or less) than the reference would predict." That is a statement about detection of a list. It is not, on its own, a statement about the process:

  • The list is shared. Most signalling genes sit in many pathways (in KEGG, the G protein subunit GNAS is in 53 of 372 human pathways, the kinase PRKACA in 72 and the G protein GNAQ in 50), so a high "pathway" score can come from genes that are on for unrelated reasons.
  • Big lists drown out the gene you care about. If a 226-gene pathway contains one adenosine receptor, the other 225 genes decide the score. Section 2 counts this.
  • Sparse data is mostly zeros. In a typical cell most list genes read zero, and a zero can be a sampling zero or a "not listed" zero (see the zero page). What the score does with zeros (each method's "zero policy") is a large part of what it reports.
  • Depth moves everything. Cells where more genes were detected score higher on almost any list. Section 4 measures how much.
  • mRNA is not signalling. Having the receptor's transcript says the cell can make the receptor. Adenosine signalling also needs ligand supply, the right G protein, and effectors in the same cell. A set that lists all of these can say "this cell is equipped", never "adenosine is acting here".

Two things stand out. First, the adenosine receptors appear only in broad pathways, and the biggest, the two "neuroactive ligand" lists, are really catalogues of receptors and channels, so a score on them probably tracks how many such receptors a cell has. Second, KEGG's grouping mirrors the pharmacology: A1 and A3 (the Gi-coupled pair) share cGMP-PKG and sphingolipid signalling, while A2A and A2B (the Gs/Gq pair) share calcium, Rap1, vascular smooth muscle and alcoholism. A1 and A2A, which push cAMP in opposite directions, share only the two catalogue pathways and cAMP signalling, the one pathway where the two directions meet. No KEGG pathway is "the A1 pathway". The A1 panels used earlier on this site (8 and 61 genes) are our own hand-curated gene lists, not KEGG sets, so their results are not a statement about the paper's collection.

For every gene in the panel, census_gene_groups.py reads the Census normalized layer over all 10x primary cells (not a sample). Counts are summed per group, then here per tissue or per cell type. Each cell of the grid is (cells with a count above zero) ÷ (cells from datasets that listed the gene). Columns are the tissues or cell types with the most cells; a cell type must have at least 200 measurable cells to be coloured. The colour scale is chosen by the button: within gene stretches each row from 0 to its own maximum so a rare gene's pattern is visible; absolute uses 0 to 100% for all rows so genes can be compared with each other. Hatched cells could not be measured.

What it can't show: this is per-group detection, not per-cell co-expression. It tells you which tissues and cell types detect each gene, not whether a single cell has the receptor and its G protein and its effector at once. That needs the per-cell matrix and is the next step.

4. What a pathway score would measure here: detection tracks depth

To see what a generic pathway score reflects, take the thirteen KEGG pathways above and ask, for a sample of cells, what fraction of each pathway's genes is detected. If that fraction mostly follows how many genes were detected in the cell overall, a pathway "score" would mostly be reporting sequencing depth.

How this figure was made

Scanning every pathway gene in all 89M cells is too heavy, so census_pathway_sample.py draws a stratified random sample: up to 600 cells from each (tissue, cell type, whole cell or nucleus, normal or disease) group. For every sampled cell and every pathway it counts the pathway genes with a raw count above zero and divides by the number of that pathway's genes the cell's dataset listed (so an unlisted gene is not counted as "undetected"). Cells are weighted by group size ÷ sampled in group, so tissue averages estimate the whole 10x population. The bottom line of the figure is the mean number of genes detected per cell (the Census nnz field). The correlation is computed across the tissues shown.

What the figure shows

  • Detection of any one pathway is low. In a typical cell only about 5-15% of a signalling pathway's listed genes are detected, and about 1-5% for the two "neuroactive ligand" catalogues. A score on these lists is built mostly from zeros.
  • Depth explains some of it, not all. Across the 26 tissues, detection correlates with genes detected per cell at about r = 0.34 to 0.50 depending on the pathway. That is a moderate link, so the tempting claim "pathway scores are only depth" is not supported at tissue level. But 26 tissue averages cannot separate depth from real biology, nuclei versus cells, or which datasets happen to be in the sample (heart stands out and is mostly nuclei). The clean test is within one cell type, across cells of different depth, and I have not run it.
  • Different lists answer different questions. Parkinson disease is detected far more than the signalling pathways. I have not checked its membership, but KEGG's Parkinson map is known to include many mitochondrial and ribosome-adjacent genes that nearly every cell expresses, so a high score there says "this cell is metabolically active", not "dopaminergic". This is the "list is shared" point from section 1 in the data.

5. Before trusting any gene-set score, ask

  1. What is in the list, and how big is it? If your gene of interest is one of 200, the score is not about it.
  2. What is it compared against? Other genes in the cell, other cells, or random control genes. That reference is also what can fool it (see the four scenarios).
  3. What does it do with zeros? And how many of the zeros are "not listed" rather than "not detected"?
  4. Are the compared cells equally deep? If not, match depth before comparing.
  5. Would random gene sets of the same size and expression do the same thing? That null check is the next item in the A1 roadmap.
