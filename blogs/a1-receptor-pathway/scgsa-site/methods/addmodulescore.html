Magnitude · vs control genes

AddModuleScore

Average the gene set's expression, then subtract the average of random genes that are expressed at similar levels. Whatever is left is the set's activity beyond what its expression level alone would predict.

The question it asks

A raw average of a gene set is dominated by how highly its genes are expressed in general: a set of housekeeping genes always "wins". To get at activity, you need a baseline that controls for expression level.

AddModuleScore (from Tirosh et al. 2016, now Seurat's default) builds that baseline per gene: for every gene in the set, draw random genes with a similar average expression, and subtract their mean. A set that is high only because its genes are highly expressed scores near zero. A set that is high for its expression level scores positive.

The computation

  1. For every gene in the dataset, compute its average expression \(\bar{x}_g\) over all cells.
  2. Sort genes by \(\bar{x}_g\) and cut them into 24 bins with equal numbers of genes.
  3. For each set gene \(g \in S\), sample 100 genes from its bin, without replacement. Pool and de-duplicate them into a control set \(C\).
  4. For each cell \(c\):
$$\text{score}_c = \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{g \in S} x_{gc} \;-\; \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{g \in C} x_{gc}$$

On our toy cell

With only ten genes the binning is illustrative, but the arithmetic is the same. Say B and D sit in a high-expression bin whose controls turn out to be A and F, while G sits in a low bin whose controls are C, E and I:

$$\underbrace{\tfrac{7.42 + 6.91 + 0}{3}}_{\text{set mean } = \,4.78} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{8.29 + 7.76 + 0 + 0 + 0}{5}}_{\text{control mean } = \,3.21} \;=\; 1.57$$

The zero on G counts as a zero, just like the zeros among the controls. Nothing special happens to it.

What the benchmarked code does

The paper's script calls AddModuleScore(object, geneSets) with every default: nbin = 24, ctrl = 100, seed = 1, on the data (log-normalised) layer. From Seurat 5.5.1's source:

data.avg <- Matrix::rowMeans(assay.data[pool, ])
data.cut <- cut_number(data.avg + rnorm(length(data.avg))/1e30,
                       n = nbin, labels = FALSE, right = FALSE)
ctrl.use[[i]] <- c(ctrl.use[[i]], names(sample(
    data.cut[which(data.cut == data.cut[features.use[j]])],
    size = ctrl, replace = FALSE)))

Two details the paper doesn't mention. The control pool is not purged of the set's own genes, so a set gene can be its own control. And the seed is reset on every call, so the same gene set always gets the same controls.

Zero policy, and what leaks in

Zeros: ordinary values. They lower the average like any small number.

From outside the set: the control genes, and the bins that choose them. The bins are cut on averages taken over both groups, so anything that reshapes the overall expression distribution — a group of extra genes appearing in half the cells — moves bin boundaries and changes which controls get drawn. And if signal genes crowd one bin, a gene set's controls can themselves be signal genes, cancelling part of a real signal or inventing a fake one.

Seen in the playground

In a toy scenario 1 where only half of the dense genes carry the signal, AddModuleScore falsely flagged most gene sets made of unchanged genes (85%). When all dense genes carry the signal, as in the paper, that drops to about 5%, matching the paper. The difference is whether an unchanged dense gene's controls come from a bin full of signal genes.

On our A1 data

AddModuleScore was the only method behind the first version of the A1 analysis, and in a simplified form: scored once per cell type from averaged expression, which the paper never benchmarked. Scored properly, per cell:

  • On the 8-gene panel, Hippocampal CA4 ranked 2nd of 21 HBCA superclusters, behind Upper rhombic lip. On the 61-gene panel it ranked 5th.
  • Its ranking moved the most between the two panels of any method (Spearman ρ = 0.39).
  • Upper rhombic lip detects the fewest genes per cell in HBCA (median 1,987, vs. 7,604 for CA4). In those cells the controls, mostly moderately expressed genes, are often zero while the dense panel genes still register — a plausible reason it tops this method. More on this.
