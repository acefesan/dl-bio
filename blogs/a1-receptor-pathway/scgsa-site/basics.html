Foundations

Scoring from zero

Everything the seven methods share: what the data looks like, why zeros are the whole problem, and how the paper decides whether a method "found" something.

1. The raw material: a sparse count matrix

Single-cell RNA-seq gives a matrix of counts: rows are genes, columns are cells, and each entry is how many mRNA molecules of that gene were captured from that cell. A typical droplet experiment captures a few thousand molecules per cell out of perhaps 20,000 genes, so most entries are zero. In the paper's sparse scenario, the median signal gene is detected in only 1.75% of cells.

Throughout this site we use one toy cell with ten genes, and the gene set S = {B, D, G}:

geneABCDEFGHIJ
count12503070102

The cell captured 30 molecules in total. Gene G is in our set but reads zero.

Notation used on every page
  • \(g\) is a gene and \(c\) is a cell. \(G\) is the total number of genes (10 in the toy cell, roughly 20,000 in real data).
  • \(S\) is the gene set being scored, and \(k = |S|\) is the number of genes in it (here \(S = \{B, D, G\}\), \(k = 3\)).
  • \(\text{count}_{gc}\) is the raw count of gene \(g\) in cell \(c\). \(x_{gc}\) is the same entry after the normalisation below. All logs are natural logs.
  • A score \(s_c\) is the single number a method assigns to cell \(c\) for the set \(S\).

2. Normalisation: making cells comparable

Two cells with identical biology can differ tenfold in total counts just because one droplet captured more. Every method in the paper runs on Seurat's LogNormalize output (the data layer), which divides by the cell's total, scales to 10,000 and takes a log:

$$x_{gc} = \log\!\left(1 + \frac{\text{count}_{gc}}{\sum_{g'} \text{count}_{g'c}} \times 10^4\right)$$

Here \(\text{count}_{gc}\) is the raw count of gene \(g\) in cell \(c\), and \(g'\) is a dummy index that runs over all \(G\) genes, so the denominator is the cell's total count. For our cell, gene A becomes \(\log(1 + 12/30 \times 10^4) = 8.29\), gene B becomes 7.42, and every zero stays exactly 0.

Keep this in mind

The denominator is the cell's total. If some genes rise, every other gene's normalised value falls, even though nothing happened to it. That coupling is why the paper's real-world simulation adds its signal after normalisation, and why the playground has a switch to add it before, so you can watch the leak.

3. Zeros: the whole problem in one word

A zero can mean two different things:

  • Biological zero — the gene really isn't expressed in this cell.
  • Sampling zero (often called dropout) — the gene is expressed, but none of its molecules happened to be captured.

From a single cell you cannot tell which. If a gene truly makes up a fraction \(p\) of a cell's mRNA (its share of all molecules in the cell) and you capture \(N\) molecules in total, the expected number of captured molecules of that gene is \(Np\), and the chance of seeing zero is roughly \(e^{-Np}\) (the Poisson probability of a count of 0). For a gene at 1 molecule in 10,000 in a cell captured at 3,000 molecules, that is \(e^{-0.3} \approx 0.74\): a three-in-four chance of reading zero even though the gene is on.

So every method has to decide what a zero means. That decision is its zero policy, and it turns out to predict a lot of the method's behaviour. The side-by-side page tabulates all seven.

4. Ranks, and the tie problem

Four of the seven methods (AUCell, UCell, ssGSEA, JASMINE) throw away the expression values and keep only the order of genes within each cell. Ranking our toy cell from highest to lowest:

geneAFBDJHCEGI
rank1234567, 8, 9, 10 — all tied at 0

The four zeros are tied, and the method has to decide where G goes. Real cells have thousands of tied zeros, so this is not a detail:

  • Average ties (UCell, ssGSEA): every zero gets the same average rank, here 8.5.
  • Random ties (AUCell): each zero gets a random rank among 7–10, re-drawn per cell. This adds noise.
  • Drop the zeros (JASMINE): rank only the six expressed genes, and treat "how many set genes are expressed" as separate evidence.
Why ranks are fragile

A rank describes a gene relative to every other gene in the cell. If a cell expresses 250 extra genes, some of them land above your set's genes and push their ranks down, although your genes' expression did not change. That is exactly scenario 3.

5. From one score per cell to "detected"

A method gives each cell a number for the gene set. The paper then asks whether the numbers differ between the two groups of cells (control vs. treatment) with a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The test pools both groups' scores, ranks them, and asks whether one group's ranks are systematically higher:

$$W = \sum_{c \in \text{treatment}} \operatorname{rank}(s_c) - \frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2}$$

Here \(s_c\) is cell \(c\)'s score, \(\operatorname{rank}(s_c)\) is its position when all cells from both groups are sorted by score (smallest = 1), \(n_1\) is the number of treatment cells and \(n_2\) the number of control cells. Under no difference, \(W\) centres on \(n_1 n_2 / 2\). The test is two-sided: it fires whether treatment scores go up or down. That matters later, because some artifacts push scores down.

With many gene sets tested at once, p-values are adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. A set counts as "identified" when its adjusted p-value is below 0.05.

Power cuts both ways

With 200 cells per group, the Wilcoxon test detects tiny systematic shifts. It cannot tell whether a shift is biology or artifact. Anything that nudges all treatment-cell scores in one direction, however slightly, will be "detected". This is the key to the "bigger gene sets are shadier" question.

6. How the paper grades a method

The recovery rate is the fraction of simulated gene sets a method identifies. Here \(p\) is the Wilcoxon p-value of a gene set, adjusted by Benjamini–Hochberg across all sets tested, and \(\#\{\cdot\}\) means "the number of":

$$\text{recovery} = \frac{\#\{\text{gene sets with BH-adjusted } p < 0.05\}}{\#\{\text{gene sets simulated}\}}$$

The same number means opposite things depending on what the gene sets contain:

  • When every gene in the set was given signal (noise 0%), recovery is sensitivity: higher is better.
  • When no gene in the set was given signal (noise 100%), recovery is the false-positive rate: anything above about 5% is the method seeing something that isn't there.

Table 1 of the paper reports both, at gene set size 100 and 200 cells per group. The side-by-side page reproduces it.

7. Two kinds of simulated data

  • SSD (Splatter-simulated data): synthetic counts from the Splatter package, with parameters fitted to a real 10x dataset (GSE164381, from a study of B-cell repertoires in COVID-19, Hoehn et al. 2021). Sparser, with a lower signal-to-noise ratio.
  • RWSD (real-world simulated data): the real, log-normalised GSE164381 matrix, cells randomly split into "control" and "treatment", with signal added to chosen genes in the treatment half.

RWSD usually gave better results. Each setting was replicated 10 times.
