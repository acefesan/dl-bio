Census (SOMA) · spatial

Where the adenosine receptors sit in tissue sections

The Census also carries a separate spatial collection: tissue sections where every measurement has an x and a y. This page lets you open every one of its 540 sections and see where ADORA1, ADORA2A, ADORA2B and ADORA3 were detected, spot by spot. As on the other Census pages, a gene a dataset never counted is shown as "not measured", never as a section full of zeros.

The honest headline first

There is no brain and no spinal cord in the spatial part of this Census release (2025-11-08), in either organism, and the brain is where ADORA1 lives. The closest thing is 74 sections of whole early mouse embryos (Theiler stage 14, about day 9) with their neural tube, and there all four receptors are almost silent. So this page cannot show the A1 receptor in the place the rest of this site cares about. What it can show is ADORA2A lighting up the thymus, ADORA1 favouring the atria over the ventricle of the heart, and ADORA2B in gut, kidney and lung.

1. What spatial data is in the Census

The spatial collection lives next to the dissociated one, at census_spatial_sequencing/{homo_sapiens, mus_musculus}. It uses two technologies, and they are very different kinds of measurement:

  • Visium (10x Genomics, "Spatial Gene Expression V1"): a glass slide printed with about 5,000 spots, each 55 µm across with centres 100 µm apart. A tissue section is laid on top, and each spot captures the RNA of whatever is above it, typically somewhere between one and ten cells. Each Visium dataset also comes with a photograph of the stained section (H&E).
  • Slide-seqV2: a "puck" tiled with 10 µm barcoded beads, roughly one cell across, so each bead is close to a single cell, but each bead catches far fewer transcripts. No tissue image comes with these.

How these numbers were made

census_spatial_adora.py export_all (in projects/caffeine/lab/001_adora_expression/) opens the local Census with tiledbsoma and reads the whole obs table of each spatial experiment. One dataset is one tissue section ("scene"), and every row of obs is one Visium spot or one Slide-seq bead. A Visium slide records every spot on the slide, including the ones with no tissue above them; obs flags those with in_tissue = 0 and the script drops them. Slide-seq has no such flag, so every bead is kept. "Spots" in the tiles is what remains. "Tissues" counts distinct values of the Census's coarse tissue_general column, and "collections" counts the published studies the datasets belong to.

The table's last two columns are per-spot medians taken first within each dataset, then the median across that assay's datasets: raw_sum is the total number of RNA molecules (UMIs) a spot captured and nnz is the number of distinct genes with a count above zero. They show how much each assay sees per spot. Every one of the 540 scenes has spot coordinates (spatial/<dataset>/obsl/loc), every Visium scene has an H&E image (img/…/hires), and none of the Slide-seq scenes does. All of these numbers are read from the one index file the viewer below also uses, so the page and the maps can never disagree.

How this table was made

For each dataset and each receptor the export asks the Census presence matrix (ms/RNA/feature_dataset_presence_matrix) whether that dataset listed the gene at all. If it did not, the dataset contributes nothing to that receptor's column, not a pile of zeros. If it did, all its in-tissue spots are "measurable", and the script reads the raw count layer for just the four receptor genes and counts the spots with a count above zero. The table then sums measurable spots and detecting spots over all datasets of a tissue (limited to the organism and assay chosen with the buttons) and divides. So a tissue's number is pooled over its spots, which means big sections weigh more than small ones. Cells are hatched when fewer than 500 spots could have measured the gene, because a percentage from a handful of spots is noise. "Most detected" names the receptor with the highest of the four percentages, among those with at least 500 measurable spots. Shading is scaled per column, so it compares tissues within one receptor; use the numbers to compare receptors. Rows are sorted by the receptor chosen under "Rank by". "Endocrine gland" is the Census's coarse group for the thymus.

Two things make this different from the dissociated heatmap on ADORA across the Census. A Visium "spot" is a little patch of tissue, so a spot detects a receptor if any of the several cells in it contributed a molecule; spot percentages are therefore not comparable with cell percentages. And the two assays differ by more than tenfold in molecules per spot, so pooling them mostly measures the mix of assays, which is why the buttons keep them apart by default.

Every dot is one tissue section (dataset); its position is the % of its in-tissue spots detecting the receptor. Square-root scale, so small values are spread out. Red-filled dots are the featured sections; the outlined one is the section open in the viewer. Click any dot to open it.
How this figure was made

The same per-dataset numbers as the table, without pooling. Each row is a fine-grained Census tissue term (for example "sinoatrial node" rather than "heart"), limited to the organism and assay chosen above, and only datasets that listed the gene are drawn. The number on the right is the pooled percentage of the row. The x axis is the square root of the percentage so that the many sections between 0 and 5% do not pile up on one pixel. Hover a dot for the dataset title, the number of spots and the exact percentage. This is the figure to check before trusting any single map: several tissues have sections that disagree with each other by a factor of five or more.

3. The maps: every section

Pick any section. Each dot is one in-tissue spot or bead at its real position. In detected mode a dot is coloured if the spot caught at least one molecule of the chosen receptor and grey if it caught none. In expression level mode the coloured dots are shaded by CP10K (see below), from the lowest nonzero value in the section to its 99th percentile. Drag to pan, use the buttons, a pinch, a double click, or the mouse wheel (after clicking the map, or with Ctrl held) to zoom. Hover or tap a spot to read all four receptors in it.

How this map was made

census_spatial_adora.py export_all goes through all 540 datasets in turn. For each one it reads the scene's spot table (spatial/<dataset_id>/obsl/loc: one row per spot with x, y in pixels of the original full-resolution image, plus the spot's soma_joinid), joins it to obs to get each spot's total molecule count (raw_sum) and the in_tissue flag, drops off-tissue spots, and reads the raw count of the four receptor genes for exactly those spots. Only nonzero entries are stored in the Census, so every spot not returned has a count of zero. Nothing is smoothed, imputed or interpolated: one dot is one stored spot, and the export checks that every in-tissue spot has a position.

To keep 4.9 million spots small enough for a static web page, each section is one compact binary file, fetched only when you pick that section. Positions are stored as 16-bit integers spread over the section's bounding box, which is a rounding error of well under a micrometre at any size in this collection (Visium spots are 55 µm). Each spot's total molecule count is stored exactly, and for each receptor only the spots with a count above zero are listed, with their count. A typical (median) Visium section is about 16 kB plus a JPEG of about 125 kB, and a typical Slide-seq puck about 100 kB (the largest, 44,588 beads, is 262 kB). All 540 sections together are about 70 MB: 30 MB of spot files and 40 MB of H&E photos.

Detected means a raw count of at least one. CP10K divides the receptor's count by the spot's total molecules and multiplies by 10,000. The page computes it from the two stored integers with exactly the expression the exporter uses, which is the same normalisation as the dissociated Census's normalized layer (the spatial collection only ships raw counts). CP10K lets a spot that captured few molecules overall weigh its single receptor molecule more, which also means one stray molecule in a thin spot can look "high"; that is why the colour scale caps at the 99th percentile of the section's nonzero values instead of the maximum. Colour intensity is therefore relative to the section; compare sections with the percentages in the table, not with colour.

The dot size is the real spot size: the Census stores each scene's spot radius (soma_geometry), so Visium dots are 55 µm discs at 100 µm spacing. Slide-seq beads are tiny at full view, so detected beads are drawn at least about 4 pixels across and undetected ones at least 1 pixel; zoom in to see them at their true size. Grey dots are drawn first, then coloured ones from the lowest value to the highest, so the highest spots sit on top.

The H&E background is the Visium "hires" image stored with each scene, cropped to the in-tissue spots plus a small margin, downscaled to at most 1,000 pixels on the long side and saved as a JPEG. The Census stores the image's pixels in (channel, x, y) order (data_axis_permutation = [2, 1, 0]) and a pure scale factor from spot coordinates to image pixels; the exporter reorders the pixels to (y, x, channel), asserts that the transform has no rotation, shear or offset, and records which rectangle of spot coordinates the JPEG covers. The page then draws the photo into exactly that rectangle under the spots. Read the other way round, the in-tissue spots land on blank glass, which is how the axis order was checked. Use the slider to fade the photo; it is a light pink slide in both colour themes.

4. What the maps show

These notes come from the featured sections (the quick links above the viewer) and from the table and strip plot, which cover every section.

  • The thymus is the brightest adenosine-receptor tissue in the spatial Census, and it is ADORA2A. In the featured 3-month-old human thymus ADORA2A is detected in 80% of in-tissue spots (1,976 of 2,481), and thymus is that receptor's top tissue: all 31 human thymus sections (from two studies) detect it, from 7% to 82% of spots. In the map the signal fills the densely packed lobules rather than tracing a thin structure, which is what you would expect if developing T cells (thymocytes), the bulk of the organ, carry it. Detection rates overlap between fetal and postnatal sections (medians 47% and 41%), but the amount per spot does not: the median postnatal section reads 1.6 CP10K against 0.6 for fetal ones. The other three receptors are close to absent in the same spots (ADORA3 4%, ADORA1 and ADORA2B under 1%).
  • ADORA1 prefers the atrium to the ventricle. In donor D6, the left-atrium section detects ADORA1 in 12% of spots against 2% in the same donor's left ventricle, from the same study and the same fresh-frozen protocol. Across all 23 fresh-frozen atrium, ventricle, septum and apex sections from that heart study, the eight atrial sections read 3.6 to 12% and the ventricle and septum sections 1 to 4%, with one exception (an apex section at 9%). This matches the textbook picture (A1 receptors in atrial muscle and the nodes slow the heart), but with detection this low a single section is weak evidence; the strip plot is the stronger version.
  • The sinoatrial node section is mostly a chemistry story. The three sinoatrial node sections from donor AV1 were made with the FFPE (probe-based) Visium protocol and read ADORA1 in 33 to 37% of spots and ADORA2A in about 17 to 20%. The eight fresh-frozen sinoatrial node sections from other donors read about 6% and 0.1%. An anatomical difference that large, all in one donor and one protocol, is more likely the probe panel being more sensitive for these genes than a real tenfold difference in the node. The map is still worth a look: in the FFPE section ADORA1 is spread broadly over the section, denser on the right-hand side than over the pale, loose tissue on the left, rather than concentrated in one small patch (I have not matched it to the authors' annotation of where the node itself is).
  • ADORA2B sits in gut and lung. The featured rectal cancer section detects ADORA2B in 21% of spots and the mouse lung in 35%. In the dissociated Census ADORA2B is the epithelial receptor (colon epithelium, bladder urothelium); mouse lung has 8 Visium sections, all between 18 and 35%.
  • The embryo is nearly silent. In the featured E9.5 mouse embryo (Slide-seqV2, 15,452 beads) the receptors are detected in 84, 45, 71 and 4 beads respectively, all under 1%. An earlier pass with the authors' per-bead cell-type labels (not part of this viewer's files) found the few ADORA1 beads mostly in the embryonic heart region ("cardiac valve cell", 39 of 84) and among "paraxial cell" beads, and only 6 among the 3,132 "spinal cord interneuron" beads. So at this stage the neural tube does not detectably make ADORA1, which fits A1 coming up later in brain development; with a median of about 2,700 molecules per bead it could also simply be missed.
  • Everything is sparse, again. Outside the thymus, a "high" section is one where a receptor is seen in 10 to 35% of spots, and the spots that see it nearly always hold one or two molecules. In expression-level mode, the colour differences between lit spots are mostly the difference between one molecule and two, divided by how many molecules the spot caught in total.

5. Caveats

  • No brain, no spinal cord. Neither organism's spatial collection has a single brain or spinal cord section, so the A1 receptor's home tissue is simply not here.
  • A Visium spot is not a cell. Each 55 µm spot mixes the RNA of several cells, often of different types. A lit spot says "some cell under here gave a molecule", not which one. A lit lobule in the thymus could be thymocytes, or the stromal cells between them. Slide-seq beads are closer to single cells but capture far less.
  • Spatial assays see fewer molecules and fewer genes. A Slide-seq bead holds a median of a few hundred molecules from a few hundred genes; a Visium spot holds thousands of molecules but they are shared among several cells. A low-abundance receptor transcript is easily missed, so the percentages here are a floor, and not comparable with the dissociated-cell percentages on the other pages.
  • A grey dot means "not detected", not "absent". Zero molecules of a receptor in a spot is entirely compatible with the receptor being made there at a low level. Where a dataset did not list a gene at all, it is "not measured" and excluded, never drawn as zeros. This matters most for human Slide-seq, where many datasets did not list ADORA2A or ADORA3.
  • Protocols differ. Fresh-frozen Visium captures polyadenylated RNA; FFPE Visium uses a fixed panel of gene probes, and its sensitivity per gene is different. The sinoatrial node sections are the clearest example in this collection: the FFPE ones read ADORA1 several times higher than the fresh-frozen ones.
  • Coverage is narrow. Much of the human collection is disease tissue (kidney disease, cancers, myocardial infarction, Sjögren's), and the mouse collection is mostly kidney and early embryo. Use the "normal / disease" filter to keep them apart.
